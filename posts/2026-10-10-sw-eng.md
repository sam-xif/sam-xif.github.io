In late 2022, ChatGPT was released, and this has since snowballed into the
largest economic bet in the
[history of the United States](https://www.wsj.com/economy/the-ai-build-out-is-becoming-the-biggest-economic-bet-in-u-s-history-c60716dd?eafs_enabled=false).
If you had asked me then whether I thought software engineering might be
automated, I might have said "probably, but not for a while, and not before many
simpler white-collar tasks."

Many of us probably thought similarly, but as it turns out, it might be the
thing that AI *is best at.* And there are a few good reasons for it:

1. There is a lot of training data
1. The industry has generally converged on well-known and accepted patterns and
   principles for system design
1. Revelant annotations, comments, discussions and other metadata about software
   are more likely to be on the internet and in a computable form than in other
   industries
1. There are very clean reward signals: the build fails, tests fail, an API call
   is failing, etc.

Today, the New York Times reported on the
["verification tax"](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/10/10/business/dealbook/ai-verification-tax-rework.html?eafs_enabled=false)
of AI-assisted work. I think this is an underappreciated limiting factor on how
much more economic growth we can really squeeze out with AI; if we truly want to
unlock extreme productivity gains, we need to be able to 1) actually have the AI
doing just as good or better than humans, and 2) be able to trust the AI to do
the job well in a variety of situations. Perhaps the case is different in
industries across the economy, but in software, I have found that both of these
criteria are satisfied, depending on the quality of the task definition.

As I recently wrote about in my
[announcement of `ruby-lean`](/blog/topics/devlog/2026-09-26-ruby-lean.html), I
ended up having to scrap many thousands of tokens' worth of development work
several times. However, I was able to get the agents to perform really well when
I imposed a rigid structure of how trustworthy progress can be made. And
throughout the entire project, agents have performed far better than me at
writing Lean proofs that check and conform to the rules I impose.

So, in pure software domains like
[`ruby-lean`](https://github.com/sam-xif/ruby-lean), at least, I have been able
to unlock superhuman progress. This extends to my day job at Stripe as well.
Stripe, along with every other large tech company, are implementing their own
[software factories](https://x.com/businessbarista/article/2061573150719021542),
where agents autonomously define implementation plans, write code, test it,
review it, get it merged, and monitor it in production. Humans move a level up,
setting the direction. Gergeley Orosz posted about
[OpenAI's](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/openai-software-factory)
software factory. Different companies are at different levels on the scale of
full factory production of software; it makes sense that the frontier labs like
OpenAI and Anthropic would be at the forefront. Anthropic
[claims](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement) that as
of May 2026, 80% of code that gets merged into the codebase is authored by
Claude.[]^(Anthropic also points out that 8x more lines of code per quarter are being shipped now compared to 2021-2025. I don't really find this all that significant though because lines of code is not the best measurement of actual outcomes delivered.)

In other domains, similar gains can be made by implementing a scaffold of checks
and balances that build robust monitoring frameworks and compounding trust as a
result.

All this is to say that productivity gains from AI are *real*, and improving,
even if they have not diffused throughout the economy yet. Because the feedback
loop of software engineering is so tight, and because new training data gets
generated every day as coding agents are extensively used by software developers
around the country and world, I expect the productivity gains will continue to
increase.

## What does this mean for software engineers?

Software engineering is already in a precarious position.
[Software engineering job postings on Indeed](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IHLIDXUSTPSOFTDEVE)
in the US are up compared to last year around this time, but still down
tremendously from the boom years around the pandemic. Furthermore, The Pragmatic
Engineer
[reports](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-job-market-in-2026-part-2)
that just 1 in 10 software engineering hires is a new graduate, down from 1 in 3
in 2023. The number of computer science graduates also has
[peaked](https://www.reveliolabs.com/news/social/computer-science-has-hit-its-high-water-mark)
this year, meaning that more new CS graduates than ever are entering a market
that is getting automated away, with an eroding salary premium. The same linked
article reports that enrollment in other STEM professions like engineering and
finance are increasing now, with computer science likely to be on a steep
downward trajectory as the salary premium erodes and agents get better at it.

I think this is just the beginning. Entry level work will of course be the first
to get automated, and it largely already is; I find myself being able to close
out bug tickets within an hour or less by delegating an agent to it, whereas
last year I might have assigned that ticket to a new hire as a task to help them
get ramped up with the code. So not only are entry level roles harder to get
hired for; once hired, there are fewer opportunities to develop skills that
would get a junior engineer to the next level.

As AI capabilities improve, I believe this will spread upwards towards the
higher levels of the engineering career ladder. In the short term, there will be
insane unmet demand for senior engineers who can steer the agents with a refined
understanding of the code so that the system grows sustainably...until the
agents develop that refined understanding.

Here is how I picture it playing out: not one cliff, but a series of
phase-delayed waves.

```wavechart
range: 2023-01 | 2030-07
today: 2026-10
spike: 2025-01 | 2026-04
caption: An illustrative model, not data. Mouse over the chart to get a sense of how I'm thinking about this.
tier: Entry level | 2026-12 | 8
tier: Mid level | 2027-08 | 12
tier: Senior | 2028-03 | 16
tier: Staff | 2028-11 | 20
note: 2023-01 | all | Before the spike | Each line is one level of the career ladder, indexed to its own peak (100). Early models assist engineers more than they replace any of them.
note: 2025-01 | all | Unmet demand spikes | Agents make software dramatically cheaper to build, so companies want to build far more of it, and they need engineers at every level to steer the agents. This is the *insane unmet demand* of the short term.
note: 2026-08 | 0 | Entry level falls first, and fastest | The work handed to new grads is precisely scoped and specified. AI is best at this kind of work. Entry-level demand drops off a cliff. The starter tickets that used to ramp new hires up go to agents in companies' software factories instead.
note: 2026-10 | all | Where we are today | Everything to the right of this line is projection (dashed). Demand is still near its peak at most levels, but entry level has started to fall.
note: 2027-02 | 1 | Mid level follows | About eight months behind, mid-level demand falls, a little less steeply. Coding agents reliably ship multi-file features, and fewer juniors are being promoted into this tier because it becomes progressively harder to keep up with agents that can do simple tasks better and faster.
note: 2027-07 | 2 | Senior falls once agents can maintain and refactor | Senior engineers ship complex, impactful projects and deeply influence the code's design and evolution. As AI develops a similarly refined understanding, with scaffolding to provide agents inputs from GTM and observability systems to drive product engineering direction, senior demand falls too.
note: 2027-12 | 3 | Staff holds out longest | Setting technical direction across many systems is the hardest thing to hand off, so staff demand falls last and slowest. But it still falls as agents earn trust.
note: 2029-06 | all | Near zero by 2030 | By 2030, demand at every level is close to zero. What is left is a small group of people who set direction, define tasks precisely, and own the trust that lets everyone else rely on the agents' work.
```

A few things could invalidate this theory. First, AI capability improvement
could plateau, though I find this unlikely. Second, harkening back to my
[first blog post](2026-03-31-ai-stall.html), AI could get more expensive as
capital infused into effective inference subsidies dries up, growth in AI output
*quality* could also stall due to thinning R&D budgets, or growth in inference
speed/compute availability could stall. Finally, I think the most
underappreciated risk is macro risk. COVID-19 demonstrated how quickly and
easily our supply chain can be shocked. The Iran war continues to keep energy
prices in flux. A larger-scale global conflict could put a pause on advanced AI
development indefinitely.

But at that point, we have way bigger things to worry about than the job market
for software engineers...

## What does this mean for the software/SaaS economy?

The important quantity is

$$
\frac{Q}{C \cdot T}
$$

Read: the ratio between output **Q**uality and the product of **C**ost and
**T**ime it takes to produce the amount of tokens required to achieve an average
task. This is pretty hand-wavy and deserves a much more rigorous analysis, but
as a simple starting point I think it suffices. I dub it the <b>"build
payoff."</b>[]^(I'm open to suggestions for a better name.)

Build payoff, as the name suggests, controls the "build-vs-buy" tradeoff. If
cost of tokens and time to generate them asymptotically approaches zero, while
quality holds steady or increases, even at current frontier capability levels,
this dramatically shifts the calculus away from purchasing SaaS products that
other companies are making, and towards building everything in-house. But this
is an idealized scenario. In reality, the cost and time consumed by inference
for tokens will remain elevated for some time, and this means that SaaS
companies will continue to capture the value of the agentic software factory
boom: agents and their "steerers" will decide that it is better to buy an
observability platform (that other agents are building and maintaining, under
the direction of the shareholders).

You may wonder: how can build payoff be measured? Quality can be measured in a
few ways. One that jumps to mind is METR's
[task horizon benchmark](https://metr.org/time-horizons/), although the latest
update to their webpage notes that measurements of task horizon length above 16
hours are "unreliable" with their current set of benchmark tasks. Another way
might be the number of "successful" agent rollouts, as measured by Anthropic in
their
[recursive self-improvement post](https://www.anthropic.com/institute/recursive-self-improvement).
Cost and time are more straightforward. These are measured already on platforms
like [Arena](https://arena.ai/leaderboard) and
[OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/rankings).

The build payoff is still being held down mainly by cost. Heavy AI use can rack
up bills upwards of several thousand dollars a month. Anthropic's models are
notoriously expensive and slow as well, with OpenAI models currently favored for
their lower cost and higher inference speed. The quality does continue to rise,
however.

I think it is pretty safe to conclude that cost won't come down soon too.
Anthropic recently
[filed for IPO](https://www.reddit.com/r/Anthropic/comments/1wvqd07/anthropics_figures_for_the_ipo/)
with financies that indicate a staggeringly high operating loss, almost twice
its revenue.

<img src="/blog_assets/anthropic.webp" />

Put another way, building software that meets a user need will still require
risk, capital outlays, and a business model. I think this is why we are
observing a
[RenaiSaaS](https://www.stripeeconomics.com/p/the-saaspocalypse-was-more-like-a),
contrary to investors' fears of a "SaaSpocalypse" at the beginning of this year.
Don't get complacent though. As the build payoff increases, value will cease to
be captured by SaaS companies, because it will become so much easier to build
complex things quickly.

## How to prepare

Honestly, I'm still trying to figure this one out. A part of me hopes what I'm
anticipating here doesn't come to fruition, but I am not holding my breath. I
feel that the writing is already on the wall.

Here are a couple principles I'm trying to align around right now:

1. **Do what you love.** Easier said than done, given your financial standing,
   but if you're able, find a way to work on something you're truly,
   differentially passionate about. I'm hoping to take my career in this
   direction soon.
1. **Stay human.** Build rich social networks, and maintain critical, nuanced,
   thought in the face of an AI slop firehose.

Thank you, dear reader, for getting this far. Stay well and safe!
